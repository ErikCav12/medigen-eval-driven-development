Eval-driven development for the MediGen synthesis engine: 5 principles to peek into the Black Box

Imagine an F1 car with no track, no finish line and no barriers, just raw power on open ground. Everyone watching is blown away because it's fast and loud and looks incredible. But there's no goal, no way to know if it's even heading in the right direction, and without the fanfare of the track to notice, eventually it silently crashes into a mountain.

Now put that same car on a track with the same engine and the same speed. But here, the finish line tells you when you've won, the barriers nudge you back when you drift and keep you pointed in the direction of the finish line, and the telemetry tells you what to tune between laps.

That's the difference between an AI system built without evals and one built with them. It allows you to peek into the black box inner workings of an LLM. For MediGen, where researchers rely on the synthesis engine to pull cited answers from a corpus of 50,000 clinical and competitive intelligence documents, a polished demo is not enough. You have to go beyond shipping LLM-powered features that look great in demos but have no measurable definition of success and no way to catch failures before a researcher bases a decision on them.

Eval-driven development plants the synthesis engine firmly on the track. Here are five principles that map across the AI development lifecycle, from understanding the research problem through to production.

Principle 1 | Define success before you build

Most teams start by building the pipeline and worry about measuring quality later. This is backwards. If you can't express what "good" looks like as a test, you don't understand the problem well enough to build a solution. This principle ensures that evals are your guide from the beginning, and not just a tick box QA exercise.

For the MediGen synthesis engine, the question is sharper than it looks: what does a good research answer actually look like? It answers the researcher's actual question rather than an adjacent one. Every claim is grounded in a specific passage from the corpus, not paraphrased or interpolated. Citations resolve to document, page, and section. The tone matches the register a senior researcher expects: confident where the evidence is strong, cautious where it is thin, and never speculative. None of this is vague aspiration. Each one is a testable criterion. Define these first, and every decision that follows has something to measure against.

If you can't test for it, you can't trust the build.

Principle 2 | Defence in depth

Even with a clear definition of success, one test at the end of your pipeline isn't enough. LLM systems have multiple stages, and each stage can fail differently. A retrieval step can pull the wrong documents. A generation step can hallucinate from the right documents. A safety layer can miss edge cases. If you only evaluate the final output (task based), you'll know something went wrong but not where. So, you need both end-to-end evals as well as "turn based" evals, which measure the outcome of each. Typically, there are three types of evals that cover these; code-based evals, LLM as a Judge and Human Review. The same measuring suite can also surface performance issues like latency and cost per call, giving you operational visibility alongside quality.

So let's show this in practice by returning to the MediGen synthesis engine. It has a retrieval layer, a generation layer, and a safety layer, and each one needs its own eval. Did retrieval pull the right passages from the 50,000-document corpus? Did generation stay faithful to those passages, or interpolate between them? Did the safety check catch the engine inventing a study outcome that does not appear in any source document? Did the answer match the tone a senior researcher expects: confident where the evidence is strong, cautious where it is thin? Think of it like Swiss cheese: the holes in one slice get caught by the next.

An important concept here enabled by AI is the LLM as a Judge, where a separate model evaluates the outputs of the workflow model. The obvious question is how you validate the validator. If you're using an LLM to judge whether answers are faithful to the source passages, how do you know the judge itself is right? Typically, it makes sense to use a frontier or fine-tuned model that is potentially more expensive to keep scores in check. But you still need confidence in your judge. One approach is to run it against a set of human-labelled examples from MediGen's research team and track its true positive and true negative rates. Calibrate your judges the same way you'd calibrate any clinical instrument, because if you don't validate them, these judges are about as useful as a chocolate teapot.

Principle 3 | Make success binary

So you know where to place your evals. Now the question is what each one should actually look like. And here's where it is tempting to fall into the objectively measured 5 step criteria per eval. However, what does a 3 out of 5 mean when you're rating a synthesis answer about KRAS-G12C? "Okay but not great?" You'll find that two MediGen researchers rarely agree on what a 3 means, and an LLM judge is even less consistent. The scale feels precise but it measures nothing useful.

This is the "God Evaluator" anti-pattern, where a single prompt asks an LLM to score everything at once. It's unreliable and impossible to debug because the actual signal gets buried in noise.

The fix is simpler than you'd think: ask yes or no. "Did the answer cite a real passage in the corpus? Yes/No." "Does every numeric claim resolve to a specific source? Yes/No." "Does the register match how a senior researcher would write it? Yes/No." Each question gets its own evaluator. Binary decisions are much more reliable than Likert scales for both human reviewers and LLM judges, and if you need more granularity, you add more binary questions rather than stretching one question across five levels.

Three binary evals you trust will always beat one score out of five you don't.

Principle 4 | Understand real failures

Moving into production territory when you are monitoring eval scores, and one drops... how do you diagnose and fix it?

This is where error analysis comes in. Read actual traces: the full record of what the system did from input to output with every intermediate step. This starts from day one, not after months in production. The moment your system produces its first outputs, go beyond the dashboards and start reading.

When you read traces, you'll see two kinds of error. The first is a specification failure: your instructions were ambiguous, so the model did exactly what you asked, just wrong. The fix is a prompt edit, not model tuning.

The second is a generalisation failure: the instructions were clear but the model couldn't handle a new input it hadn't seen before. The fix is more examples or fine-tuning. Confuse these two and you'll waste weeks tuning a model when the real problem is a two-line prompt edit. Either way, the burden sits with the Applied AI team, not the customer.

The synthesis engine has been live for two weeks and your evals are flagging that answers about CPD-4821 dosing are failing more often than other topics. You could tweak the prompt and hope for the best. Or you could pull 100 failing traces and actually read them. When you do, a pattern jumps out: the engine isn't hallucinating randomly. It's consistently quoting the pilot-study dose (200mg BID) as the standard dose, even when researchers ask about expanded-cohort or compassionate-use patients, which run on a different protocol. Clearly, that's not a model problem. The prompt never distinguished the pilot cohort from the expanded cohort, and retrieval is happily pulling the pilot protocol for every CPD-4821 query. No amount of model tuning fixes a broken spec. This is a specification failure. Fix the ambiguity first, then measure whether the fix holds on new inputs.

Back to our F1 analogy, don't tune the engine when the map is wrong.

Principle 5 | Keep measuring

Shipping with evals isn't the finish line. It's the starting line. Production will always surface failures that no pre-launch test suite can anticipate, and you need two practices running continuously to stay ahead of them.

The first is regression tests for known failures. Run your golden dataset on every commit. These are the failure modes you've already discovered and fixed, and regression tests make sure they stay fixed. For the synthesis engine, this catches when a prompt update accidentally breaks the CPD-4821 dosing answers you fixed last month.

The second is production monitoring for new failures. Sample live traces, run your judges on them, and track success rates over time. This catches what your regression suite doesn't know to test for. The engine's tests all pass, but a new compound was added to the corpus last Tuesday with an interim safety addendum not yet indexed. Production monitoring is what catches the engine confidently synthesising an answer about a study that wasn't in the corpus when the evals were written.

Automated evals will inevitably drift. Hence the importance of reviewing traces, live examples of how researchers are using the engine. At some point, a human reviewer will notice that the engine is technically correct but weirdly overconfident when evidence is thin, something no binary eval was originally designed to catch. That observation becomes a new eval, and the whole cycle continues. Regression tests catch what you've seen before, production monitoring catches what you haven't, and humans notice what neither can.

The track doesn't slow you down

Spending significant development time on evals can feel like overhead, extra work that delays shipping the MediGen synthesis engine. But without them, you ship fast once and then spend months debugging in production with frustrated researchers as your test suite. With evals, you ship with confidence and iterate faster because you know exactly what's working and what isn't.

These five principles change how MediGen builds, but they also change how MediGen scopes projects, how the team decides what to ship, and how external vendors get evaluated. When someone pitches you an AI solution, ask them where their evals are. If the answer is "we'll add those later," you're looking at an F1 car with no track.

Build the synthesis engine for the track its researchers will be racing on. That's how you earn their trust and keep it.
